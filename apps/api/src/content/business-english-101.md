# Writing email that gets a reply

Most professional email fails because it asks too much of the reader. The remedy is **subject lines that promise**, **first lines that deliver**, and **one clear ask**.

Subject lines are micro-summaries, not titles. "Quick question" is useless; "Need budget approval for Q3 hires by Fri" tells the reader the topic, the action, and the deadline before they click. First line should restate the ask explicitly — never bury the request after two paragraphs of context. The reader is on a phone, scrolling between meetings; they will not scroll.

Keep emails to five sentences when you can. If you need more, use a TL;DR at the top: "Asking for approval on X by Friday. Details below." Bullet points beat paragraphs for anything with three or more items. End with one specific call to action: "Can you confirm by 5pm Thursday?" — not "let me know your thoughts."

# Meeting English: agendas, status, decisions

A meeting without an agenda is a hangout. Send the agenda 24 hours ahead with three columns: **topic**, **owner**, **time-box**. People come prepared. Discussions stay scoped. Meetings end on time.

When giving status, use the **traffic light**: green ("on track, no help needed"), amber ("on track but watch X"), red ("we have a problem; here's what we need"). Skip the storytelling — leaders want signal, not journey. If you ran tests and they passed, say "tests pass" not "we did extensive testing throughout the week and I'm pleased to report things went well overall."

End every meeting with explicit decisions and owners: "decision: ship feature X on Tuesday; owner: Priya; deadline: Monday EOD." If a decision wasn't made, name that too: "no decision on pricing; we'll bring data next Tuesday." Ambiguity at the end of a meeting is the seed of next week's confusion.

# Presenting with structure

Western business presentations use a tight structure: **opening hook**, **three main points**, **summary**. Each main point is one slide with one idea, supported by one or two bits of evidence. Slides are not your script; they're cues for the audience. Read your slide out loud and you have already lost the room.

Use the Pyramid Principle: lead with your conclusion, then support it. "We should raise prices 15%" comes first, then the three reasons, then the data behind each reason. People can follow your argument and decide how deep they need to listen.

Avoid corporate jargon. "Synergize," "circle back," "leverage," "deep-dive" — they sound like communication and aren't. "Let's combine these teams" is better than "let's synergize the teams." Plain words land harder.

# Cross-cultural awareness

Business English is spoken by more non-native than native speakers globally. Adapt your speech for this reality. **Speak slowly** (not loudly) when on calls with mixed nationalities. **Avoid idioms** — "ballpark figure," "low-hanging fruit," "boil the ocean" don't translate. **Confirm understanding** explicitly: "to make sure I have this right, you want X by Y — correct?" — much better than assuming agreement from a polite "yes."

In many Asian and Indian business cultures, "yes" can mean "I hear you" rather than "I agree." If a decision feels too easy, probe with "what concerns do you have about this?" The disagreement that comes out in private email afterwards is the same disagreement that wasn't surfaced in the meeting — better to find it now.

# Negotiation phrases that work

Three phrases punch above their weight. **"Help me understand…"** ("help me understand the reasoning behind the timeline") is non-confrontational but extracts the actual constraint. **"What would have to be true for this to work for you?"** flips the conversation from positions to interests. **"I can do X if you can do Y"** is the only way to make trade-offs explicit without sounding transactional.

When you don't have an answer, **don't fill silence with words you'll regret**. "Let me think about that and come back to you tomorrow" is professional and protects your position. The pressure to respond immediately is internal; the other side will almost always wait.
