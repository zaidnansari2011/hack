# Resume: the 8-second skim test

Recruiters give your resume an average of 8 seconds on the first pass. Optimise for that scan, not for the third reading. Your name and intended role are at the top. The most recent job is first. Each role gets 3–6 bullets, each bullet starts with an action verb, each bullet quantifies impact whenever possible.

"Worked on the dashboard" is dead text. "Reduced dashboard load time from 8s to 1.2s, used by 3000 daily users" is alive. Quantify: percentages, rupees, users, time saved. If you cannot put a number on it, can you put a comparison? "Owned the migration from PostgreSQL 12 to 15 with zero downtime." Numbers are how the reader's brain converts your words to credit.

One page if you have under 8 years of experience. Two pages if you have more. Three pages signals "padded" — the senior people I trust most have one-page resumes with confidence to spare. Save it as PDF (Word files render differently on every machine), name it `Firstname-Lastname-Role.pdf`, not `resume_final_v4.pdf`.

# Cover letters: short, specific, sincere

The cover letter most candidates write is generic, wordy, and self-focused. The cover letter that gets read is short (under 200 words), specific to the company, and reader-focused. Three paragraphs.

**Para 1:** Why this company, not just any company in this industry. "I read your engineering blog post on X and noticed Y." This proves you did 10 minutes of homework. **Para 2:** Two concrete examples of work you've done that maps to what they're hiring for. Not your full job history — the two most relevant data points. **Para 3:** One sentence on the next step. "I'd love a 30-minute call this week to discuss."

If you can't write something specific about why this company, don't send a cover letter. A blank field is better than "I am writing to express my interest in the position…"

# Interview prep: research, stories, questions

Three things to prepare before any interview. **Company research** — recent funding, recent product launches, who you'd be working with, what the team's stack is. Spend 30 minutes minimum. **Your stories** — 6–8 concrete project stories from your past, each tagged with the skills they demonstrate (leadership, debugging, conflict, ambiguity, scope cuts). When asked "tell me about a time…", you should already have the story ready, not be inventing on the fly. **Your questions for them** — at least five thoughtful questions about the team, the codebase, the roadmap, the metrics. Interviewing is a two-way evaluation.

Practice your "tell me about yourself" out loud. 90 seconds. Past → present → future arc. "I started as X, currently doing Y at Z, looking to move into A because B." Read this answer to a friend; the awkward bits are what your interviewer will notice.

# Behavioural questions: the STAR shape

The most common interview format in 2026 is behavioural — "tell me about a time when X happened." Answer in the **STAR** shape: **Situation** (1–2 sentences of context), **Task** (your specific responsibility), **Action** (what you did, in detail — this is the longest part), **Result** (what happened, with numbers where possible).

The mistake most candidates make is spending too long on context and too little on action. The interviewer wants to know what *you* did, not what the team did. "We" is a yellow flag; "I" is what they're listening for. "We migrated the database" — fine. "I led the design, did the cutover at 2am, and rolled back when we hit the issue with foreign keys" — that's the substance.

End with what you learned. "Looking back, I'd do X differently because Y." Self-awareness is a hard-to-fake signal of seniority and is increasingly part of what interviewers evaluate.

# Negotiation: get the offer in writing first

Never accept on the phone. "This is great news — can you send the details in writing so I can review carefully?" buys you 24–48 hours to think. Most candidates accept the first number they hear; they leave 10–25% on the table.

Negotiate the whole package, not just base salary. Joining bonus, stock vesting, performance bonus structure, notice period, leave policy, work-from-home expectations, equipment budget. Many of these are easier to move than base because they don't reset the company's salary band.

The phrase that works: "I'm very excited about this role. To make the move from my current position, I'd need to see the base at ₹X." A specific number, not a range, anchored by a real reason (your current comp, market data, the cost of the move). Then stop talking. Silence after a number is the most powerful tool in negotiation.
