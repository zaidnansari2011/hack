# The greenhouse effect in one paragraph

Sunlight reaches Earth's surface as short-wavelength radiation, warms the surface, and is re-emitted as longer-wavelength infrared. Certain gases in the atmosphere — CO₂, methane, water vapour, nitrous oxide — absorb that infrared and re-radiate it in all directions, including back down. This is the greenhouse effect; without it, Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18°C instead of +15°C. The problem isn't the effect itself — it's the rate at which we've been amplifying it.

Atmospheric CO₂ was around 280 ppm before industrialisation. It's now over 420 ppm — higher than any point in the last 800,000 years of ice core records. The correlation between CO₂ and global temperature in those ice cores is what makes climate scientists confident this isn't natural variability.

# Why a 1.5°C rise matters

A 1.5°C global average increase sounds small. The reason it matters is that **averages hide tails**. A warmer average means heat waves that were once-in-a-century become once-in-a-decade. Hot summer days in India that hit 45°C used to be rare events; they're becoming annual. The shift in the distribution at the extreme is far more violent than the shift in the middle.

Specific consequences at 1.5°C vs 2°C (from IPCC AR6): tens of millions more people exposed to severe drought; coral reef cover declines 70–90% vs 99%; an additional 10 million people at risk from sea level rise; loss of 1.5× more vertebrate species. The half-degree difference is not academic.

# The carbon cycle and feedback loops

CO₂ released from fossil fuels enters a global cycle: roughly a quarter is absorbed by oceans, a quarter by land vegetation, and half stays in the atmosphere. The atmospheric portion has a long tail — about 20% of CO₂ emitted today will still be in the atmosphere in 1000 years.

The dangerous part is **feedback loops** that could accelerate warming beyond what current emissions predict. Melting Arctic sea ice exposes dark ocean, which absorbs more heat (albedo feedback). Thawing permafrost releases trapped methane (a much more potent greenhouse gas in the short term). Warming oceans absorb less CO₂. These feedbacks aren't speculation; they're observed and partly modelled into current projections, but they introduce significant uncertainty on the high end.

# Mitigation: what actually moves the needle

Personal carbon footprints are real but small compared to systemic changes. The largest leverage points, globally: **electricity generation** (decarbonising the grid via renewables + storage + nuclear), **transport** (electrifying cars, buses, two-wheelers), **industrial heat and cement**, **buildings** (insulation, heat pumps), **land use** (reforestation, regenerative agriculture).

For an individual in India, the highest-impact changes are: how you travel (long-haul flights have outsized impact per kilogram of CO₂), what you eat (beef and lamb are 10–20× higher in emissions than poultry or plants), home energy (efficient AC, switching to renewables where the grid allows), and political voice — climate policy is shaped by what voters demand.

# Adaptation: living with what's coming

Even if we hit net-zero by 2050, we're committed to roughly 1.5°C of warming. Adaptation isn't defeatism — it's prudence. For India specifically: heatwave action plans (early warning, cooling centres in urban slums), water-secure agriculture (drought-tolerant crops, drip irrigation, groundwater management), flood-resilient infrastructure in coastal cities, vector-borne disease surveillance (warmer climates expand the range of dengue and malaria mosquitoes).

The cost of adaptation rises super-linearly with each tenth of a degree. ₹1 spent on prevention saves ₹6 in adaptation, which saves ₹40 in disaster response. Climate is no longer a future scientific problem; it is a present infrastructure and public-health problem with budget consequences in every Indian state.
