# Stress, anxiety, depression — what's the difference

These three words get used interchangeably and shouldn't be. **Stress** is the body's response to a specific demand or pressure — usually time-limited, often useful in short bursts. **Anxiety** is anticipation of future threat, real or imagined, often without a clear trigger; it persists when the threat has passed. **Depression** is a sustained low mood that interferes with daily functioning — sleep, appetite, work, relationships — typically lasting two weeks or more.

Stress can become anxiety can become depression if it isn't addressed. None of them is a character flaw. They are health conditions with biological underpinnings (cortisol, serotonin, neuroinflammation) and respond to interventions ranging from sleep and exercise on the mild end to therapy and medication on the more severe end.

# Recognising signs in yourself

The honest checklist for the past two weeks: Are you sleeping enough, but waking unrefreshed? Has your appetite changed noticeably? Have you withdrawn from people you usually enjoy? Are tasks that were easy now exhausting? Do you feel disconnected from things you used to care about? Are you irritable at small things? Are you using alcohol, food, or screens more than you'd like to numb feelings?

If three or more are yes and have lasted two weeks, that's a signal — not a diagnosis, but a signal that warrants attention. The earlier you act, the smaller the intervention needed. Waiting until you can't get out of bed makes recovery harder, not heroic.

# Talking to someone who is struggling

The most useful thing you can offer is presence without judgement. Ask **open questions** ("how have you been, really?"), **listen without solving**, and **believe what they tell you**. The instinct to cheer up ("things will get better!") or compare ("at least you don't have…") shuts conversation down — it tells them their feelings are inconvenient.

Three sentences that help: "That sounds really hard." "I'm glad you told me." "What would feel useful right now?" The last one matters — sometimes they want advice, sometimes a distraction, sometimes just for someone to sit with them. Don't assume.

If someone mentions thoughts of suicide or self-harm, take it seriously. Ask directly — "are you thinking about hurting yourself?" Asking does not plant the idea; it shows you can handle the answer. Then help them reach a crisis line or accompany them to an emergency room. India has iCall (9152987821) and Vandrevala (1860-2662-345) as free helplines.

# What actually helps — the boring basics

The interventions with the strongest evidence are unsexy. **Sleep** — 7–9 hours, consistent times, no screens an hour before bed. **Movement** — 30 minutes of walking or any aerobic activity, most days; effect size on mild depression rivals medication. **Sunlight** in the morning, particularly important in winter. **Connection** — at least one meaningful conversation a week, in person or on a call. **Reduced alcohol and stimulants** — both worsen anxiety and sleep.

For more than mild symptoms, **therapy works**. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) has the largest evidence base for anxiety and depression. Six to twelve sessions can produce real change. Online options have made access cheaper in India (MindPeers, YourDOST, Amaha). For moderate-to-severe conditions, **medication** combined with therapy outperforms either alone — a psychiatrist (not a general physician) is the right starting point.

# Reducing stigma at work and home

The single biggest barrier to people seeking help is fear of being seen as weak. Reduce stigma by treating mental health like any other health: take a day off when you need to without elaborate cover stories, share your own experience when appropriate, ask "how are you doing — really?" and mean it.

For managers and parents, normalise the basics. Ask about workload before asking about output. Notice changes in colleagues or children early — withdrawal, irritability, declining work. A 10-minute conversation in week one is worth more than a crisis intervention in month three. Mental health is health. The sooner the culture you're in agrees, the easier it gets for everyone.
