# Budgeting and the 50-30-20 rule

A budget is just a plan for what your money will do before it arrives. The simplest framework that works for most salaried Indians: spend up to 50% of take-home pay on needs (rent, groceries, utilities, transport, insurance premiums), up to 30% on wants (dining out, entertainment, subscriptions, travel), and route at least 20% into savings and debt repayment. The percentages aren't sacred — they're a starting point you adjust as your income grows.

The hardest part isn't math; it's tracking. Open a notes app or a spreadsheet and write down every rupee leaving your account for one month. Patterns jump out: the ₹400 weekly Swiggy habit is ₹20,000 a year; the unused ₹999/month subscription is ₹12,000. Awareness creates choice. Automate the savings transfer the day your salary lands so the 20% is gone before you can spend it.

# Emergency fund first

Before investing, before paying off non-EMI debt, before anything, you need an emergency fund: 3 to 6 months of essential expenses sitting in a savings account or liquid mutual fund. The point isn't returns; it's that an unexpected medical bill, a job loss, or a parent's hospitalisation doesn't force you to sell investments at a loss or take a 36% personal loan.

Park it somewhere boring and accessible — a high-yield savings account (6–7% in many private banks) or a liquid debt fund. Don't put it in equities or fixed deposits with lock-in periods. The job of this money is to be there in 24 hours when something breaks. Returns are a bonus, not the goal.

# Old vs new tax regime

Since FY 2023-24, salaried Indians choose between two income tax regimes each year. The **old regime** has higher slab rates but lets you claim deductions: 80C (PF, ELSS, life insurance, home loan principal — up to ₹1.5L), 80D (health insurance), HRA, LTA, standard deduction, home loan interest. The **new regime** has lower slab rates and almost no deductions, only the ₹50,000 standard deduction.

The break-even is roughly: if your eligible deductions total more than ₹2.5–3 lakhs, the old regime usually wins. Below that, the new regime is simpler and often cheaper. Run the numbers in any free online calculator each year — don't pick once and forget; your deductions change with rent, marriage, kids, and home loans.

# Investing: index funds and SIPs

For 90% of people, the right investment is a low-cost equity index fund — a Nifty 50 or Sensex tracker — bought through a Systematic Investment Plan (SIP) that auto-debits ₹5,000 or ₹10,000 a month. Why: low expense ratios (0.1–0.3% vs 1.5–2% for actively managed funds), broad market exposure, no need to pick winners, and SIPs handle rupee-cost-averaging — you buy more units when prices are low.

The math that makes this work is compounding. ₹10,000/month at 12% annual return becomes ₹1 crore in 20 years. The same SIP started 10 years later becomes ₹23 lakhs. Time in the market beats timing the market — start with whatever you can, even ₹1,000/month, and increase it every year with your salary hikes.

# Insurance: term + health, not endowment

Insurance is for catastrophic risk, not investment. Buy **pure-term life insurance** (online, no broker, premium <₹15,000/year for ₹1 crore cover at age 30) only if someone depends on your income. Skip endowment, ULIPs, money-back, and "guaranteed return" plans — they bundle insurance with terrible-return investments and pay agents huge commissions.

For health insurance, ₹5–10 lakh cover for a family is a reasonable floor. If your employer provides cover, buy a personal ₹5L floater on top — job changes leave you exposed for the gap month, and pre-existing conditions get harder to insure as you age. Health insurance is non-negotiable in a country with no public safety net for serious illness.
