# Recognising an emergency

The hardest first-aid skill is admitting something is wrong. Bystander hesitation kills more people than ignorance does. A useful rule: if you find yourself thinking "should I call an ambulance?" — call. The 102/108 dispatcher will help you decide, and the call itself doesn't commit you to anything.

Three signs that always justify an emergency call: **chest pain or pressure** lasting more than a few minutes, especially with sweating or nausea; **sudden one-sided weakness or slurred speech** (the F.A.S.T. check — Face droop, Arm weakness, Speech slurred, Time to call); **severe difficulty breathing**. Each of these has a treatment window measured in minutes; every minute of delay reduces the chance of recovery.

# CPR and the AED

Cardiac arrest survival drops about 10 percentage points per minute without CPR. The simplified protocol every adult should know: **check for response**, **call for help**, **start chest compressions** — centre of the chest, 5–6 cm deep, 100–120 per minute (the tempo of "Stayin' Alive" by the Bee Gees). Compressions only — if you're untrained in rescue breaths, skip them. Hands-only CPR is nearly as effective for the first 8 minutes and far easier to do.

Don't stop until paramedics take over or the person revives. CPR is exhausting; if there are two of you, swap every two minutes. AEDs (automated external defibrillators) are increasingly common in airports, malls, gyms. They speak instructions out loud — switch it on, follow the voice, and trust it. The device decides whether to shock; you can't shock someone who doesn't need it.

# Bleeding and wound care

Stop bleeding with **direct pressure**, applied firmly with a clean cloth, and held for at least 10 minutes without peeking. Most wounds will clot if you give them this chance. Elevation helps for limbs. Tourniquets are for catastrophic limb bleeding only — they're not appropriate for everyday cuts and can cause harm if misused.

For everyday wounds: rinse with clean water (not hydrogen peroxide — it damages tissue), pat dry, apply an antiseptic cream and a sterile dressing. Tetanus boosters every 10 years; sooner if the wound is deep or contaminated. Wounds that gape open more than a centimetre, won't stop bleeding, or are caused by animal/human bites need a clinic — not a home dressing.

# Choking and burns

For a choking adult who can still cough — let them cough; their own reflex is more effective than any intervention. If they can't speak or breathe and are turning blue, start **back blows** (five firm slaps between the shoulder blades) alternating with **abdominal thrusts** (Heimlich — fist above the navel, sharp inward-and-upward squeeze) until the object dislodges or they lose consciousness, at which point start CPR.

For burns: cool with running water for 20 minutes. Not ice (which causes further damage), not butter or toothpaste (which trap heat and create infection risk). Cover loosely with cling film or a clean cloth. Burns larger than the patient's palm, burns on the face/hands/genitals, and any burn on a child or elderly person need medical assessment. Don't pop blisters — they're sterile.

# Public health: prevention beats treatment

The greatest gains in human longevity came not from medicine but from public health: clean water, sanitation, vaccination, food safety. As an individual, the highest-leverage health habits are unglamorous and free: don't smoke, sleep 7–8 hours, walk 30 minutes daily, eat mostly plants, stay current on vaccines.

In community terms, three behaviours protect those around you. **Hand washing with soap** for 20 seconds prevents diarrhoeal disease — still a leading killer of children under five in India. **Vaccination** protects the immunocompromised and infants who can't be vaccinated, through herd immunity. **Staying home when ill** during respiratory outbreaks is a small inconvenience that prevents hospitalisations and deaths in vulnerable groups.
