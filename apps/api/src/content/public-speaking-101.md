# Stage fear is normal — and useful

The shaking hands, the dry mouth, the racing heart — these are not signs you're not cut out for public speaking. They are evolved threat responses to "being watched by a group," and they happen to almost every speaker, including professional ones. The goal isn't to eliminate the response; it's to perform well despite it.

A useful reframe: anxiety and excitement are the same physiological state. Your body doesn't know the difference between "I'm terrified" and "I'm pumped." Studies have shown that telling yourself "I am excited" out loud, instead of "I am nervous," measurably improves performance. The energy is the same; the label changes what you do with it.

# Structure: the three-act talk

The single biggest improvement any beginner can make is **structure**. Audiences forgive everything except being lost. A reliable three-act shape works for talks from 5 to 50 minutes:

**Act 1 — set the stakes.** Open with a problem, a story, or a striking statistic. The audience should know within 90 seconds why they should keep paying attention. **Act 2 — the journey.** Three main points, each illustrated by a story or concrete example, building on each other. **Act 3 — the payoff.** The implication, the call to action, the question you want them to keep thinking about. Echo the opening so the talk feels whole.

Avoid the academic structure ("first I will discuss…, second I will discuss…") for talks under an hour. Listeners don't have your patience for setup.

# Stories beat statistics

Numbers don't move people; stories do. A statistic about 10,000 deaths from a preventable disease is forgotten; a story about one named person dying from it stays for a year. Use both: the story makes it human, the statistic makes it general.

A good story has a person, a struggle, and a turn. "I once had a student who couldn't read at age 11. Her teachers had given up. We tried a different method — six months later she was reading at grade level." The shape — character, problem, change — is what your audience's brain is wired to remember.

For business or technical talks, the story is often your own. "Here's the mistake we made and what we learned" beats "here are the lessons of project management." Specificity earns trust; generality slides off.

# Delivery: voice, pace, silence

Most amateur speakers talk too fast. Slow your rate by about 20% from what feels natural — what feels achingly slow to you sounds confident and considered to the audience. **Pause after important sentences**, not before — give the line time to land instead of rushing to the next.

Vary your pace and pitch deliberately. Monotone — same volume, same speed throughout — is the fastest way to lose a room. Drop your voice for emphasis on key words; speed up briefly for energy; pause for a full two seconds before the punchline. Two seconds feels like an eternity on stage and is exactly right from the audience's seat.

Silence is the most underused tool. When you forget a word, mid-sentence, the instinct is to fill the gap with "um" or "you know." Don't. A two-second silence reads as confidence — you're thinking, and that's allowed.

# Slides and visuals

Slides are not your script; they are cues for the audience to support what you're saying. The moment you read your slide out loud word-for-word, you've lost the room — the audience can read faster than you can talk, and they switch off. One idea per slide. A short heading and a single visual or chart usually beats a paragraph of bullet points.

Rules that hold up: high-contrast text on a clean background, font sizes large enough to read from the back row (28pt+ for body text), and no animation unless it serves the message. If a slide needs more than a few seconds to absorb, it's doing too much — split it in two. Build the slide for the listener at the back, on a phone, half-paying-attention. If it works for them, it works for everyone.

# Q&A and difficult moments

The Q&A is when most talks unravel. Three rules. **Repeat the question** so everyone hears it and so you have a beat to think. **Answer the question that was asked**, not the question you wish had been asked — audiences notice deflection. **It's okay to say "I don't know"** — followed by "I'll find out" or "what's your best guess?" Either is more credible than fabrication.

Hostile questions are a gift if handled well. Stay calm, acknowledge the point ("I can see why you'd say that"), give your honest response, and move on. Don't argue — you'll never convince the questioner and you'll look defensive to everyone else. Hostility in a Q&A almost always ends in you looking better if you don't take the bait.
